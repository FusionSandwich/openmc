# StellarCSG repository review: Have you already tried analytical plasma geometry?

Yes. Your repository already implements almost exactly the quasi-polar implicit-surface approach I suggested. Reimplementing that method from scratch would largely duplicate existing work.

I inspected your [FusionSandwich/openmc repository](https://github.com/FusionSandwich/openmc), particularly the StellarCSG implementation, its September 25–26 technical reports, and open PRs #3–#5.

The more important finding is that your existing project has two different geometry problems, and most of the difficult recent development has concerned the second:

1. Plasma boundaries: Representing an arbitrary toroidal plasma surface with an implicit radial function.
2. Magnet coils: Representing swept finite-thickness conductors and reliably computing their first ray intersections.

Your plasma representation already resembles the Hirshman–Breslau approach. Your coil-intersection code has undergone considerably more intensive correctness and performance work.

## 1. What you have already implemented

Your [StellarCSG README](https://github.com/FusionSandwich/openmc/blob/JS/stellarcsg-local-continuation-20260925/dev/stellarcsg/README.md) documents the following:

| Proposed method                       | Existing StellarCSG implementation                                  | Assessment                                  |
| ------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------- |
| Quasi-polar implicit surface          | \\(F(x,y,z)=\rho-\rho_s(\theta,\phi)\\)                             | Already implemented                         |
| Reparameterize VMEC-style surfaces    | Conversion of arbitrary poloidal parameter to geometric polar angle | Already implemented                         |
| Fourier-derived geometry without CAD  | Compilation of sampled toroidal surfaces                            | Already implemented in sampled-surface form |
| Analytic surface normals              | Analytic gradient and outward normal                                | Already implemented                         |
| Fast special-case intersections       | Exact circular-torus and shaped-axisymmetric paths                  | Already implemented                         |
| General stellarator ray intersections | Layered nearest-root search                                         | Implemented, not fully qualified            |
| Native OpenMC geometry                | Experimental `periodic-spline` surface                              | Implemented, disabled by default            |
| Eliminate splines entirely            | Direct finite-Fourier evaluation or implicit trigonometric kernel   | Not established by the reviewed evidence    |
| Near-native CSG throughput            | Performance target and benchmark infrastructure                     | Not demonstrated                            |

The key equation is already present:

\\[ \boxed{F(x,y,z)=\rho-\rho_s(\theta,\phi)} \\]

That means the principal idea of converting a complicated plasma surface into a single implicit geometric classifier is not new to your project.

The meaningful distinction is that your current implementation uses a periodic bicubic radial spline field, rather than necessarily evaluating a compact Fourier expansion directly.

A direct-Fourier alternative would therefore be a different numerical representation of an existing geometric model, not a fundamentally new surface definition.

## 3. What mathematical approach is still worth trying?

I would now focus on one specific alternative rather than revisiting the complete quasi-polar method:

Compile a low-order Fourier representation into an implicit algebraic or trigonometric surface and develop a dedicated first-root solver.

There are two possible paths.

Path A: Direct Fourier implicit surface.

Keep your established radial chart, but replace the bicubic spline evaluation with

\\[ F(R,Z,\phi)= \rho- \sum\_{m,n}a\_{mn} \cos(m\theta-nN\_{\rm fp}\phi) - \sum\_{m,n}b\_{mn} \sin(m\theta-nN\_{\rm fp}\phi). \\]

This avoids spline basis evaluation. However, the ray-intersection equation remains nonlinear, and repeated trigonometric evaluation might actually be slower than the current spline.

A performance improvement is therefore not guaranteed.

Path B: Low-degree algebraic approximations.

Represent each smooth local toroidal surface region by an implicit polynomial:

\\[ P(x,y,z)=0. \\]

A degree-four representation has a particularly appealing property: substituting a ray,

\\[ \mathbf x(t)=\mathbf x_0+t\mathbf d, \\]

produces a polynomial of degree at most four in \\(t\\).

That potentially allows a conventional CSG-like finite-degree intersection procedure.

The major difficulty is establishing whether sufficiently low-degree pieces can reproduce realistic WISTELL-D shapes with acceptable geometric accuracy, continuity, and no spurious surfaces. A single general quartic will not represent an arbitrary stellarator.

Your previous Bezier, torus-arc, and interval-root experiments are relevant negative evidence here. In particular, your September 26 synthesis records an unsuccessful quartic torus-arc candidate and a Cartesian Bezier atlas with incomplete root certification. Those should be reused as comparison baselines rather than repeated unchanged.

## 4. The benchmarks I would require

Your stated goal is more demanding than simply accelerating the current implementation. It requires both accurate stellarator geometry and conventional CSG-like tracking cost.

I would define the acceptance criteria as follows:

| Criterion              | Proposed requirement                                                                        |
| ---------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Surface fidelity       | Error bounded against the original Fourier/VMEC surface at a tolerance specified in advance |
| Correct intersections  | No missed first intersections in the adversarial bank                                       |
| Grazing rays           | Explicit handling of tangencies and nearly tangent crossings                                |
| Classification         | Same geometry for inside/outside, intersections and normals                                 |
| Performance            | At least 80% of conventional native-CSG query throughput as an aspirational goal            |
| End-to-end performance | Full OpenMC histories per second, including boundary and cell tracking                      |
| Complex geometries     | WISTELL-D plasma, offset layers and realistic winding packs                                 |

The native throughput target corresponds to

\\[ \frac{T\_{\rm proposed}}{T\_{\rm native}}\leq1.25 \\]

under an appropriate matched measurement.

A geometry representation could have substantially cheaper point evaluation without improving full particle tracking, so timing the surface function by itself would be insufficient.

## 5. What I recommend changing in the research direction

Your current project is further along than my previous literature recommendation assumed.

I would not prioritize another implementation of Hirshman–Breslau quasi-polar coordinates. You have the essential radial implicit structure already.

Instead, I would prioritize:

1. Benchmarking direct Fourier evaluation against your existing bicubic radial representation on the same plasma geometry and rays.
2. Investigating low-degree implicit algebraic patches as a genuinely distinct fast-intersection method, including reviewing your historical torus-arc and Bezier experiments first.
3. Separately finishing the certified swept-coil intersection work, because accelerating the plasma surface will not fix the winding-pack root and ownership difficulties.

One important caution: I inspected the accessible repository reports and the documented implementation, but not every historical experimental branch or every C++ source file. I can therefore confirm that the quasi-polar concept has already been implemented, but I cannot conclusively exclude an unpublished or separate-branch direct-Fourier kernel.

Bottom line: Your project has already tried the main geometry representation I suggested. The unresolved problem is finding a representation and intersection algorithm with both realistic stellarator fidelity and nearly conventional CSG computational cost. Direct Fourier tracking and low-degree implicit approximations remain worthwhile comparisons, but neither should be presumed faster until tested against your existing implementation.&#x20;
